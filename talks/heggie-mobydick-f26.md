## It's raining men!

The stories of _Doctor Atomic_ and _Moby Dick_ result in an almost entirely male main cast (in _MD_ all of the characters are male). Adams gave Kitty and Pasqualita large roles and included women in the chorus. In Heggie's _MD_, Pip is a trouser role, but otherwise it's all male voices all the time.

This drew a lot of attention to Pip—more than I think was intentional. Pip's scenes created some welcome vocal variety (to my ears).

Because gender (typically) determines voice type, an opera like _MD_ has a very different sound than one with a more mixed cast. Besides (important) issues of diversity, what are your thoughts on music theater works with less variety of voice types?

## Staging

I was wondering how the staging would handle the whale hunting scenes. I thought the first appearance of the whale boats with the projection mapping was pretty cool. Later projections (like the final Ahab hunt) felt too "cinematic" for my tastes and overwhelmed the characters.

Projection mapping, video, etc. opens up lots of staging possibilities. Do you like this or does it take away from the magic of traditional stage design? Would you feel cheated if you paid money for an opera ticket and were presented with video projections and pre-recorded audio?

(Using tech in opera opens up lots of opportunities for composers and producers.)

## Queequeg & Ishmael (a.k.a. Greenhorn)

Queequeg eventually becomes his closest friend on the Pequod and a refuge against Ahab's monoomania. This makes Queg's mysterious death especially devastating.)

## The Final Hunt

At 2:09 there is a big, grand chorus in praise of Captain Ahab as he sails off to catch the White Whale. Did it have you cheering for Ahab? Get you pumped up? Or something else because you knew the ending of _MD;o,TW_?

This scene was the most jarring departure from the source material for me. I remember the crew being broken and ambivalent by the end of the novel. They hardly had any agency at that point and were fully at the mercy of their maniac captain.

## Döhl on "Tonality"

Döhl characterizes middlebrow operas as using "modestly modern tonal music" (62). Later, he writes that these operas show audiences that "more tonal approaches cannot only be witnessed in the context of film music." (65)

I think this is an overly-simplistic framing of the tonal/atonal accessible/inaccessible divide.

First off, is modern film music _really_ as tonal as he assumes? There are very few modern film composers who come from a classical background and compose in the late-Romantic, extended-tonality style we associate with John Williams or Howard Shore. I think the tonality in film music owes much more to minimalism and pop than "common practice" tonality. The modern film composers I know are much more interested in timbre and texture than anything else.

To me, Heggie's _Moby Dick_ sounds old-fashioned not because it's tonal but because it uses a deliberately 19th-century harmonic language and musical syntax.

Döhl seems to fall into the trap of using "tonal" as a synonym for "accessible."

Thoughts?

## Opera is only a click away

Döhl 55, quoting Halford: "When the higest is as accessible to the lowest—accessible in the sense that both are only a click away—we actually have to think anew about how to walk that middle line"

Do you agree that the emergence of middlebrow is, in part, a result of the easy accessibility of all forms of media and art? (N.B. Döhl cites 1995 as the beginning of the current new opera boom, which does predate the widespread accessibility of online media.)

## So you wanna be middlebrow?

How does one become a middlebrow opera composer? (I'm genuinely curious.)

Let's say I want to write a middlebrow opera based on a famous movie (I don't). It seems like you would have to have good lawyers and Hollywood connections to even secure the rights to these intellectual properties. It's hard for me to imagine a student composer making—say—_Dune: The Opera_ and then using that to get commissions for other books-turned-movies-turned-operas.

Do these middlebrow librettists and composers already have industry connections? Is it the patrons and donors who make this happen?
